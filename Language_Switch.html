Who's Who in the Hive

Every hive runs on three castes, each built for one job.

The Queen

One queen per hive. She lays up to 2,000 eggs a day and lives up to five years — fifty times longer than a worker.

The Workers

Tens of thousands of sterile females do everything else: nursing, building, guarding, and foraging, in that order, as they age.

The Drones

A few hundred males exist for a single purpose — mating with a queen from another hive. They carry no stinger and gather no food.

One Bee, Three Careers

1

Nurse (Days 1–10)

Feeds larvae, cleans cells, and tends the queen inside the dark warmth of the hive.

2

Builder (Days 11–20)

Produces wax from glands in her abdomen and shapes it into perfect hexagons.

3

Forager (Day 21 onward)

Flies up to 8 km from the hive, visiting 2,000 flowers a day, until her wings wear out.

From Nectar to Honey

Turning flower sugar into a shelf-stable food takes the whole colony.

01

Gather

A forager drinks nectar and stores it in a second stomach, separate from the one she eats with.

02

Pass it on

Back at the hive, she regurgitates the nectar into a waiting worker's mouth, again and again, breaking down the sugars each time.

03

Dry it out

Bees fan the nectar with their wings, evaporating the water until it thickens into honey.

04

Seal it

Each finished cell is capped with a thin wax lid — honey that can outlast the hive itself.

Six Things Worth Knowing

Honey never spoils. Sealed jars over 3,000 years old have been found still edible.
Bees communicate flower locations through a figure-eight dance.
A hive can maintain 35°C year-round, even in snow.
One bee makes about 1/12 teaspoon of honey in her whole lifetime.
Bees see ultraviolet patterns on flowers invisible to humans.
About a third of the food humans eat depends on pollinators like bees.
